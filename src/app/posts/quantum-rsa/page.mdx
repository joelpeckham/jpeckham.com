## Why we’re suddenly doing number theory

This is post six of [Learn Quantum](/projects/quantum/). The last few articles were oracles and interference tricks. Today is the reason people fund quantum hardware: **RSA**.

Simon’s problem hid a period under XOR. Shor’s algorithm hides a period under ordinary addition — the smallest $r>0$ with

$$f(x+r)=f(x)$$

for a function that looks random inside each period. That period is enough to break the encryption that protects a huge chunk of the internet.<Cite n={1} />

I will not build the quantum circuit yet. First I want you fluent in the classical setup: what “mod $N$” means, the group $G_N$, RSA’s public and secret numbers, and why knowing one period lets an eavesdropper read the message *without* factoring $N$.

## Congruences, without the mystique

When I write

$$5\times 6 \equiv 2 \pmod 7,$$

I mean: divide $30$ by $7$ and the remainder is $2$. Same claim in another costume: $30-2=28$ is a multiple of $7$. The symbol $\equiv$ is “equal up to multiples of the modulus.” Plain $=$ stays reserved for exact equality.<Cite n={1} />

Working “mod $N$” means you only keep the residues $0,1,\ldots,N-1$. Multiply two of them, divide by $N$, keep the remainder. That’s the whole game. Example: $11\times 3 = 33 = 2\times 15 + 3$, so $11\times 3 \equiv 3\pmod{15}$.

A **multiplicative inverse** of $c$ mod $m$ is an integer $d$ with $cd\equiv 1\pmod m$ — multiply, reduce mod $m$, land on $1$. Not every $c$ has one. You need $\gcd(c,m)=1$ (they share no prime factors). Euclid’s algorithm finds both the gcd and, when the gcd is $1$, the inverse. That is Appendix J in one sentence.<Cite n={1} />

## The cast: $G_N$

Fix a modulus $N$. Define

$$G_N = \{ a : 1 \le a \lt N,\ \gcd(a,N)=1 \}.$$

Read it as: the leftovers $1,2,\ldots,N-1$ that share **no** prime factors with $N$. For $N=15=3\times 5$, drop multiples of $3$ or $5$, and what’s left is

$$G_{15}=\{1,2,4,7,8,11,13,14\}.$$

Multiply two members mod $N$ and you stay inside the set. Every member has a multiplicative inverse mod $N$. So $G_N$ is a group under multiplication mod $N$.<Cite n={1} />

If $N=pq$ with distinct primes $p$ and $q$, a counting argument gives

$$|G_{pq}| = (p-1)(q-1).$$

That product is Euler’s totient $\varphi(N)$ — just a name for “how many residues mod $N$ are coprime to $N$.” Bob will keep $p$ and $q$ secret, publish $N$, and use $\varphi(N)$ behind the curtain.

The **order** of $a\in G_N$ is the smallest positive integer $r$ with

$$a^r \equiv 1 \pmod N.$$

Same number, different costume: the function $f(x)=a^x\bmod N$ is periodic with period $r$, and for this special $f$ the fundamental period *is* that order.<Cite n={1} />

Lagrange (Appendix I) says $r$ divides $|G_N|$. For a prime $p$, that recovers Fermat’s little theorem: $a^{p-1}\equiv 1\pmod p$ when $p\nmid a$. For two primes, Mermin derives

$$a^{(p-1)(q-1)} \equiv 1 \pmod{pq}$$

when $\gcd(a,pq)=1$, and then the RSA identity: if $cd\equiv 1\pmod{(p-1)(q-1)}$, then

$$a^{cd} \equiv a \pmod{pq}.$$

That last line is the entire cryptographic engine.<Cite n={1} />

<MathAside title="What is a group here?">

A set $G$ with a multiply-mod-$N$ operation is a *group* if it contains $1$, contains inverses (for each $a$ some $d$ with $ad\equiv 1\pmod N$), and is closed under the product. You do not need the full undergrad course. Mermin’s Appendix I is one page of cosets leading to Lagrange’s theorem: the size of any subgroup divides the size of the group.<Cite n={1} />

The subgroup generated by $a$ is the list $a,\ a^2,\ \ldots,\ a^r$ where $r$ is the order — the smallest positive integer with $a^r\equiv 1\pmod N$. That list has exactly $r$ distinct elements, and $a^r=1$ sits in it.

</MathAside>

## RSA in one screen

Scorecard before the protocol:

| Symbol | Who knows it | Plain meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $p,q$ | Bob only | Large secret primes |
| $N=pq$ | Public | Modulus Alice encrypts with |
| $\varphi(N)=(p-1)(q-1)$ | Bob (from $p,q$) | Size of $G_N$; stays secret |
| $c$ | Public | Encoding exponent, coprime to $\varphi(N)$ |
| $d$ | Bob only | Decoding exponent: $cd\equiv 1\pmod{\varphi(N)}$ |
| $a$ | Alice (then Bob) | Message, an integer with $0\lt a\lt N$ |
| $b\equiv a^c\pmod N$ | Public (the ciphertext) | What Eve sees on the wire |

Bob picks large primes $p,q$, sets $N=pq$, and picks a public encoding exponent $c$ with $\gcd\bigl(c,(p-1)(q-1)\bigr)=1$. Using Euclid (Appendix J), he finds a secret $d$ with

$$cd \equiv 1 \pmod{(p-1)(q-1)}.$$

He publishes $(N,c)$. He hides $d$ (and $p,q$).

Alice’s message is an integer $a$ with $0 \lt a \lt N$. (For the attack below we also want $a\in G_N$ — not a multiple of $p$ or $q$. For huge primes that failure is astronomically rare; if Eve is paranoid she can gcd $b$ with $N$ first.<Cite n={1} />) She sends

$$b \equiv a^c \pmod N.$$

Bob computes $b^d \bmod N$ and gets $a$ back.<Cite n={1} />

Eve sees $b$, $N$, and $c$. If she could factor $N$, she could build $d$ the same way Bob did. Factoring huge $N$ is believed hard classically — that is the bet. Shor’s contribution is a different attack: find a *period*, then decode without factors.

Play with numbers small enough to hold in your head. Encrypt, decrypt, watch the round-trip.

## Meet the period by hand

Before quantum anything, feel the order. Pick $a\in G_N$ and keep multiplying by $a$ mod $N$ until you return to $1$. The number of multiplies is $r$.

On a classical machine, hunting that $r$ for cryptographic-size $N$ is brutal — the values hop around with no smooth “sine wave” clue.<Cite n={1} /> On a quantum machine, that hunt becomes feasible. First see the ladder on toys.

<OrderFindingDemo />

<MathAside title="Euclid in one paragraph">

To compute $\gcd(f,c)$ with $f \gt c$: replace $(f,c)$ by $(c,\ f \bmod c)$ and repeat until the remainder is $0$. The last nonzero remainder is the gcd. The same bookkeeping, run backwards, builds integers $j,k$ with $jf+kc=\gcd(f,c)$. When the gcd is $1$, $k\bmod f$ is the modular inverse of $c$ — how Bob finds $d$ mod $\varphi(N)$, and how Eve will find $d'$ mod $r$.<Cite n={1} />

</MathAside>

## Eve’s cheat code (no factors required)

Here is the attack Mermin highlights in §3.3 and Table 3.1.<Cite n={1} />

1. Eve takes Alice’s ciphertext $b=a^c\bmod N$.
2. She finds the order $r$ of $b$ in $G_N$ (the period of $x\mapsto b^x\bmod N$). That order equals the order of the plaintext $a$: the subgroup generated by $a$ contains $b=a^c$, and the subgroup generated by $b$ contains $a=b^d$, so the two subgroups match and therefore have the same size $r$.
3. Because Bob chose $c$ coprime to $\varphi(N)$ and $r$ divides $\varphi(N)$, $c$ is also coprime to $r$. So $c$ has an inverse $d'$ modulo $r$:
   $$cd' \equiv 1 \pmod r.$$
4. Then
   $$b^{d'} \equiv a^{cd'} = a^{1+mr} \equiv a \pmod N,$$
   since $a^r\equiv 1\pmod N$.

Eve never learns $p$ or $q$. She only needed a period-finding oracle plus classical Euclid.

Scrub through the attack on a toy key. Imagine the middle step is the quantum computer (posts 7–8).

## What “period finding” will mean next

So the cryptographic ask is: given $b$ and $N$, find the period of $f(x)=b^x\bmod N$ efficiently.

Classically you would sample until two $x$ values collide on the same $f$ — hopeless at scale. Quantumly you prepare a superposition over many $x$, entangle with $f(x)$, and use a **quantum Fourier transform** so that a measurement lands near a multiple of $2^n/r$. Continued fractions finish the job.<Cite n={1} />

That is the next article: [Quantum Fourier Transform & Period Finding](/posts/quantum-qft-period/). After that, [From Periods to Factors](/posts/quantum-factoring/) covers the classical reduction people usually mean by “Shor factors $N$.”

If you only care about reading RSA traffic and you already have period finding, you can skip factoring. If you want the textbook punchline “here are $p$ and $q$,” keep going.
