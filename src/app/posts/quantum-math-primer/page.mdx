## Welcome to Learn Quantum

This is post zero of [Learn Quantum](/projects/quantum/). I am walking through David Mermin's *Quantum Computer Science* for programmers who bounced off Dirac notation.<Cite n={1} /> No physics. No "imagine a particle." Lists of numbers, a few symbols, and why those symbols keep showing up.

Today is the math appendix in miniature: vectors as lists, a basis, complex amplitudes, bras and kets, and the inner product that later becomes a probability. If a symbol looks scary, open the Math side quest under it.

By the end you should read $|\psi\rangle$ without flinching, compute a tiny inner product by hand, and know that probabilities come from squared lengths of complex numbers.

## Vectors are just lists

Forget arrows for a minute. In this series a vector is an ordered list of numbers. Two numbers: a 2D vector. Eight numbers: an 8D vector. Quantum computer science lives in spaces whose size is a power of two, but that is a later convenience, not a law of math.<Cite n={1} />

Write a list as a column so the order stays obvious:

$$
|\psi\rangle \longrightarrow
\begin{pmatrix}
\alpha_0 \\
\alpha_1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Those $\alpha$ entries are the **amplitudes**: the numbers that sit in each slot of the list. For now treat them as ordinary numbers. In a few sections they become complex. Same idea, richer arithmetic.

The tall bars and angle bracket make a **ket**. The decoration $|\,\rangle$ means "this is a vector." The label inside names which one. So $|0\rangle$ is "the vector we call zero," not the number zero. $|\psi\rangle$ is a generic state.

<MathAside title="What |ψ⟩ means">
<p>Dirac wrote vectors as kets so the label could sit in a normal font. $|7798\rangle$ is easier to read than $\phi_{7798}$. Vectorial character lives in the $|\,\rangle$ wrapper, not in Greek letters.</p>
</MathAside>

Two special lists matter a lot:

$$
|0\rangle =
\begin{pmatrix}
1 \\
0
\end{pmatrix},
\quad
|1\rangle =
\begin{pmatrix}
0 \\
1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

They are the **computational basis** for one bit-sized system: the two unit axes I expand every 2-entry list against. "Basis" here means a set of reference vectors you write everything else as a mix of. "Computational" is the jargon for this particular pair, the one tied to classical 0 and 1 later.

Each has length 1 (square the entries and add: $1+0=1$). Their overlap is 0 (multiply matching slots and add: $1\cdot0 + 0\cdot1 = 0$). That pair of facts is **orthonormal**: unit length, and different ones do not overlap. Formal notation lands in a later section; the demo only needs that picture.

Click a basis vector. Watch the column light up. The punchline is the zero overlap between $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$.

<Takeaway>
A ket is a labeled column of amplitudes. $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ are the unit axes we expand everything in.

## Complex numbers without the trauma

An amplitude is usually complex: $\alpha = u + iv$. Here $u$ is the real part, $v$ is the imaginary part, and $i$ is the bookkeeping unit with $i^2 = -1$. You do not need a story about "imaginary time." You need two real dials and one rule for length.

The **squared magnitude** of $\alpha$ is

$$
|\alpha|^2 = u^2 + v^2.
$$

That number is always real and never negative. Later it becomes a probability contribution. Complex conjugation flips the sign of $v$: $(u+iv)^* = u - iv$. Bras will need that.

Drag the real and imaginary sliders. Watch the point on the plane and the bar for $|\alpha|^2$. If that bar goes past 1, a lone amplitude is already too big to sit in a legal state by itself.

<Takeaway>
Complex amplitude = two real numbers. Probability weight = $u^2 + v^2$. Conjugation flips the imaginary sign.
</Takeaway>

## Bras, kets, and the dual row

If a ket is a column, the matching **bra** is a row of conjugated amplitudes:

$$
\langle\psi| \longrightarrow
\begin{pmatrix}
\alpha_0^* & \alpha_1^* & \cdots
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Dirac's joke: bra + ket = bracket. Put them together and you get a number, the **inner product** $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle$. In plain English: conjugate the left vector's amplitudes, multiply matching slots, and sum. That number is the overlap between the two lists.

For two vectors with amplitudes $\beta_x$ and $\alpha_x$,

$$
\langle\phi|\psi\rangle = \sum_x \beta_x^* \alpha_x.
$$

When the amplitudes are real, the stars do nothing and you just multiply and add. When they are complex, conjugate the bra side first.<Cite n={1} />

<MathAside title="Inner product checklist">
<p>1. Write both vectors in the same basis.</p>
<p>2. Conjugate the left vector's amplitudes.</p>
<p>3. Multiply matching slots and sum.</p>
<p>4. Same vector with itself: $\langle\psi|\psi\rangle = \sum_x |\alpha_x|^2$, which is real and positive if the vector is nonzero.</p>
</MathAside>

## Normalization: the "unit length" rule

To **normalize** a vector is to rescale it so its squared length is 1. A state we actually use must be a **unit vector**:

$$
\langle\psi|\psi\rangle = \sum_x |\alpha_x|^2 = 1.
$$

That is not etiquette. It is the requirement that probabilities will later add to one. If your amplitudes' squares sum to $0.7$, you have not written a legal state yet. Rescale, or admit you are mid-calculation.

## Inner product as "overlap"

How much does $|\psi\rangle$ look like $|\phi\rangle$? The inner product answers. If both are unit vectors, $|\langle\phi|\psi\rangle|^2$ is the number you care about for "probability of finding $\phi$ when the state is $\psi$." The measurement post will put a name on that rule. The algebra starts here.

I keep the demo on real amplitudes so the UI stays honest. The complex rule is the same with conjugations on the bra.

## Orthonormal bases, said plainly

A set $\{|x\rangle\}$ is **orthonormal** when every member has length 1 and different members have inner product 0:

$$
\langle x | y \rangle =
\begin{cases}
1 & x = y, \\
0 & x \neq y.
\end{cases}
$$

Then any vector expands uniquely as

$$
|\psi\rangle = \sum_x \alpha_x |x\rangle,
\quad
\alpha_x = \langle x | \psi \rangle.
$$

That last line is why Dirac notation wins for this subject. The amplitude in front of $|x\rangle$ is exactly the inner product with that basis ket.<Cite n={1} />
