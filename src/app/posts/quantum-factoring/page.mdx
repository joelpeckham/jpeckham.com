## The last classical glue

You have (or can get) the order $r$ of a random $a$ in $G_N$ with $N=pq$. The [QFT post](/posts/quantum-qft-period/) was how to learn $r$. The [RSA post](/posts/quantum-rsa/) showed you can decode with $r$ alone. This article is the other headline: turn $r$ into the prime factors of $N$.<Cite n={1} />

No new quantum gates. Just Euclid, a coin-flip’s worth of luck on $a$, and a short case analysis.

## What “gcd” means here

The **greatest common divisor** $\gcd(u,v)$ is the largest positive integer that divides both $u$ and $v$. Euclid’s algorithm finds it without factoring either number: replace the pair $(f,c)$ by $(c,\ f - \lfloor f/c\rfloor\, c)$ — the remainder — and repeat until the remainder is $0$. The last nonzero remainder is the gcd.<Cite n={1} />

That is the only classical tool you need after the quantum period finder returns $r$.

## The recipe

1. Pick random $a$ with $\gcd(a,N)=1$. (If the gcd is nontrivial, you already factored $N$ — absurdly lucky.)
2. Find the order $r$: smallest positive integer with $a^r\equiv 1\pmod N$. Quantum period finding does this step.
3. Hope $r$ is **even**.
4. Compute $x = a^{r/2}\bmod N$.
5. Hope $x\not\equiv -1\pmod N$.
6. Then
   $$x^2-1=(x-1)(x+1)\equiv 0\pmod N,$$
   but neither factor is $0$ mod $N$. So $N$ splits across them: $\gcd(x-1,N)$ and $\gcd(x+1,N)$ are $p$ and $q$ (in some order).<Cite n={1} />

Why neither factor is $0$ mod $N$: $x\not\equiv 1$ because $r$ is the *least* power giving $1$, and we assumed $x\not\equiv -1$.

## When luck fails

Two abort cases — throw $a$ away and sample again:

- **$r$ odd.** There is no integer $r/2$, so you cannot form $x=a^{r/2}$.
- **$a^{r/2}\equiv -1\pmod N$.** Then $x+1\equiv 0\pmod N$, so $\gcd(x+1,N)=N$ — the whole modulus, a trivial “factor.” The other gcd is usually $1$. No split.

Mermin’s Appendix M shows that for $N=pq$, a random $a\in G_N$ succeeds with probability at least $1/2$. You do not need hundreds of trials.<Cite n={1} /> I will not reproduce the primitive-root counting argument here; the demos make the failure modes concrete.

<MathAside title="Why $(x-1)(x+1)$ helps">

You know $N$ divides $(x-1)(x+1)$, and $N=pq$ with primes $p,q$. If $N$ divides neither factor alone, the only possibility is that $p$ divides one factor and $q$ the other. Greatest common divisors peel those primes out without guessing.

</MathAside>

## How this sits next to the RSA attack

Two uses of the same period:

| Goal | What you do with $r$ |
| --- | --- |
| Read Alice’s message | Order of ciphertext $b$; invert public $c$ mod $r$; $a\equiv b^{d'}\pmod N$ |
| Publish $p$ and $q$ | Order of random $a$; even/$\,{-1}$ checks; Euclid on $a^{r/2}\pm 1$ |

Same quantum subroutine. Different classical wrapper.<Cite n={1} />
